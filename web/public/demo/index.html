Harmony exists because of open data from space agencies. For 26 years NASA’s MODIS instruments on Terra and Aqua, and then the VIIRS instruments on Suomi NPP, NOAA-20 and NOAA-21, flown by NASA and NOAA, have detected active fires worldwide. NASA’s FIRMS service publishes every detection openly. Harmony reads all of it: 658 million detections, of which 536 million pass its filters, from the standard-quality archive and the near-real-time feed. Nothing else goes in. But the record is at a hand-over. Suomi NPP stopped delivering data on 2 November 2026, and MODIS is close to retirement. The grey and orange bars are two generations of sensor, and the next slide shows why they cannot simply be joined.

3 / 7

Satellites have counted active fires since 2000, on two generations of sensors. VIIRS has finer pixels and sees smaller fires than MODIS, so in the same year it reports about four times as many hotspots. Each time another VIIRS satellite is launched the raw count steps up again: seven to eight times with two, twelve to thirteen with three. So if you append one record to the other, every place on Earth appears to burn far more from 2012. And the hand-over is happening now. Suomi NPP stopped delivering data on 2 November, and MODIS is close to retirement. Anyone comparing this fire season with the last twenty needs one consistent record.

4 / 7

Three steps. First, change the unit. Instead of counting pixels, count cell-days: a 1.4 kilometre cell that had a fire on a given day or night counts once. On the right, the same fire is one MODIS hotspot or six VIIRS hotspots, and both become one cell-day. That cuts the gap to under three times. Second, calibrate what is left, in the years when both sensors flew, with a simple ratio for each biome, region, time of day and month, and correct MODIS for the drift of its own orbit. Third, do not switch from one sensor to the other. Each sensor’s error is estimated from the data by triple collocation, and every month is a weighted combination of the MODIS record and the VIIRS record, which averages the VIIRS satellites flying, with a range calibrated on held-out years. When MODIS and Suomi NPP retire, the record continues on what is left. The calibration follows the approach of published fire-emissions datasets; the fusion and the drift correction go beyond it.

This is the live app. A responder picks an area by drawing it, typing coordinates, or choosing from a watchlist of places where fire is most unusual. The first thing on screen is the answer: here, September 2020 in the Gran Chaco was 1.4 times its normal, the fourth most active September in 26 years. Below it the page follows the questions a newcomer asks, in order, starting with the burning calendar the challenge asks for: one row per year, one column per month, all on one scale, in colours that work for colour-blind readers. Everything is computed in the browser by a Rust kernel compiled to WebAssembly, reading small static tiles, so the answer arrives in about a second. The app also reads the last week as a pace against normal through a server-side proxy, writes a brief a duty officer can read aloud, plays the record as sound for people who cannot see the chart, shows the whole planet on a WebGPU globe, and exports the hotspots behind any search as a CSV.

6 / 7

Two kinds of evidence. First, agreement. Before harmonization VIIRS reports four to thirteen times as many hotspots as MODIS. After it, the ratio sits between 0.98 and 1.03 in every year from 2013 to 2024. Holding out each year from 2012 to 2021 in turn, calibrated VIIRS alone correlates 0.944, on a log scale, with what MODIS saw at one-degree scale. A change-point test finds no significant step at the hand-over, where the raw record steps 12.6-fold. On unseen years the stated 80 percent range covers 76 percent of months, so the ranges are a few points optimistic, and the app says so. Second, a finding. Because VIIRS keeps a fixed overpass time, it can be used as a reference to measure MODIS itself. From 2012 to 2019 all four lines stay within a few percent of one: the method reports almost no change when nothing has changed. Then the orbits begin to drift. By 2025 Terra passes over 1.1 hours earlier, and its daytime fire record runs 42 percent low, with a 95 percent range of 37 to 47 percent. At night they run 35 percent high. Aqua’s daytime detections are unchanged. The limit is that a real shift in the hour fires burn would look the same. NASA lists the effect of this drift on fire products as under investigation, and I found no published global estimate by biome and time of day. Harmony uses it to correct MODIS before fusing it. Two honest notes: the cell-day unit and the calibration each remove a large share of the gap, and at one-degree scale fusing the sensors beats either one alone but not a plain average. Both are stated in the app.

Harmony is live at harmony.hernie.me, and the code, the method and the tests are public on GitHub. The globe on the right is from the app: every month since November 2000, rendered in the browser. The record is built entirely on NASA FIRMS data, and because it weights each sensor’s record by its measured error it carries on with NOAA-20 and NOAA-21 after MODIS retires. It is for three groups. Responders get a plain answer to “is this unusual?”, in the same unit as the last 26 years. Scientists get a validated series with its uncertainty and its limits stated. Land managers get the rhythm of a place: when it burns, how that is shifting, which years to learn from. We acknowledge the use of data and imagery from NASA’s Fire Information for Resource Management System (FIRMS), part of NASA’s Earth Observing System Data and Information System (EOSDIS). This work is dedicated to everyone who works the fire line, the dispatch desk and the long night after. I am Toh Yu Heng, team Hernie, from Singapore. Thank you.
