Two kinds of evidence. First, agreement. Before harmonization VIIRS reports four to thirteen times as many hotspots as MODIS. After it, the ratio sits between 0.98 and 1.03 in every year from 2013 to 2024. Holding out each year from 2012 to 2021 in turn, calibrated VIIRS alone correlates 0.944, on a log scale, with what MODIS saw at one-degree scale. A change-point test finds no significant step at the hand-over, where the raw record steps 12.6-fold. On unseen years the stated 80 percent range covers 76 percent of months, so the ranges are a few points optimistic, and the app says so. Second, a finding. Because VIIRS keeps a fixed overpass time, it can be used as a reference to measure MODIS itself. From 2012 to 2019 all four lines stay within a few percent of one: the method reports almost no change when nothing has changed. Then the orbits begin to drift. By 2025 Terra passes over 1.1 hours earlier, and its daytime fire record runs 42 percent low, with a 95 percent range of 37 to 47 percent. At night they run 35 percent high. Aqua’s daytime detections are unchanged. The limit is that a real shift in the hour fires burn would look the same. NASA lists the effect of this drift on fire products as under investigation, and I found no published global estimate by biome and time of day. Harmony uses it to correct MODIS before fusing it. Two honest notes: the cell-day unit and the calibration each remove a large share of the gap, and at one-degree scale fusing the sensors beats either one alone but not a plain average. Both are stated in the app.
